# The WWWH Principle

**Formal statement:**

> Deep, transferable understanding of any concept, procedure, or phenomenon requires the learner to construct and integrate four interdependent forms of knowledge — declarative (*What*), causal–evaluative (*Why*), procedural/mechanistic (*How*), and conditional/contextual (*Where*) — and instruction should be designed so that learners must generate, apply, justify, and transfer all four, rather than recite a definition.

**Expanded statement:**

For any concept, procedure, or phenomenon **X**, a learner understands **X** deeply only when they can answer all four of the following questions and reason across their interrelations:

1. **What** — What is X? What are its parts, properties, examples, and non-examples? What is it not?
2. **Why** — Why does X exist? Why does it work the way it does? Why does it matter? What evidence, assumptions, or purposes support it?
3. **How** — How does X work? How is it used, reconstructed, or applied? How do I solve problems with it and know when I am right?
4. **Where** — Where and when does X apply? Where does it fail or not apply? Where does it connect to other concepts, contexts, and domains?

**As a learning goal:**

A learner has achieved the WWWH Principle for **X** when they can, without relying on a verbatim definition:

- state and explain **what** X is and is not;
- justify **why** X exists, works, and matters;
- demonstrate **how** X operates and is used;
- identify **where** X applies, fails, and transfers;
- and do so in their own words, with examples, counterexamples, novel applications, and boundary conditions.

**As a pedagogical rule:**

When teaching or studying any topic, do not stop at the definition. Require all four questions to be asked, answered, connected, and applied. If one is missing, understanding is incomplete: *What* alone gives inert verbal knowledge; *Why* alone gives abstract opinion; *How* alone gives unjudged procedure; *Where* alone gives context-bound mimicry.

**Qualification:** This is a pedagogical principle — a prescriptive heuristic for learning and instruction — not an empirical law. Its components are grounded in established constructs: declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge, with *Why* functioning as causal–evaluative knowledge.
