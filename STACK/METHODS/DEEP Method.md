# The DEEP Method

**A slow, elaboration-driven method for building transferable understanding—the deliberate opposite of SPRINT.**

Where SPRINT builds a recallable skeleton in 25 minutes, DEEP builds a flexible mental model over days. It is designed for conceptual and procedural knowledge, where the goal is to explain *why*, apply to unseen problems, and know the boundaries of a concept.

---

## 1. Start With Knowledge Type

Deep study fails most often because people apply the wrong method to the wrong kind of knowledge. Diagnose first:

| Knowledge type | What it is | What deep study requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Factual** | Terms, dates, labels | Recall is usually enough—don't over-invest |
| **Conceptual** | Theories, principles, models | Elaboration, comparison, non-examples |
| **Procedural** | Skills, methods, techniques | Deliberate practice, error diagnosis, varied conditions |
| **Metacognitive** | Knowing when and how to use what you know | Self-monitoring, strategy selection, reflection |

If the material is factual, DEEP is overkill—use SPRINT. If it is conceptual or procedural and you need transfer, DEEP is mandatory.

---

## 2. The Four Phases

Run DEEP in **spaced sessions of 45–90 minutes across multiple days**, mixing related concepts rather than blocking them.

### Phase 1: Deconstruct

**Goal: see the structure, not the surface.**

- State the underlying principle or mechanism in your own words from memory.
- Map the parts and how they relate—what causes what, what depends on what.
- **Produce a non-example**: a case that looks similar but doesn't belong. You don't understand a concept until you can draw its boundary.

*Output: a from-memory structural diagram plus one non-example.*

### Phase 2: Elaborate

**Goal: explain why, not just what.**

- Repeatedly ask *"Why is this true?"* and *"Why not otherwise?"* Answer from memory first, then check.
- Walk through the concept step by step, explaining to yourself why each step follows.
- Connect it explicitly to something you already know.
- Compare it with a nearby concept and name the critical difference.

*Output: a written why/how explanation from memory, plus a comparison.*

### Phase 3: Organize

**Goal: embed the concept in a network, not store it as an isolated fact.**

- Build a concept map from memory, then correct it. Prioritize meaningful connections over neat visuals.
- Generate several examples from *different* contexts—one example is memorization, varied examples build abstraction.
- Reorganize the material into your own structure: a hierarchy, a decision tree, a matrix.

*Output: a from-memory concept map or organizer.*

### Phase 4: Transfer

**Goal: prove the understanding is flexible.**

- Solve problems you have **not seen**, with different surface features. Practice identifying *which* concept applies before solving.
- Explain it to an imagined novice without notes.
- Create something new with it: an original example, a novel application, or a critique of a flawed use.
- State when the concept does **not** apply.

*Output: one successful novel-problem application plus a teaching explanation, both without source support.*

---

## 3. Bloom's as a Progression, Not a Checklist

The phases map onto Bloom's levels, but the point is that **each phase demands a higher cognitive process than the last**:

| Phase | Bloom's levels |
|---|---|
| Deconstruct | Understand → Analyze |
| Elaborate | Understand → Analyze → Evaluate |
| Organize | Apply → Analyze |
| Transfer | Apply → Evaluate → Create |

If you can only *remember* and *understand*, you have not finished. Deep understanding is reached when you can **analyze, evaluate, and create** with the concept.

---

## 4. Evaluation

DEEP is judged by performance under conditions that require flexibility—not by time spent.

| Criterion | Pass looks like | Failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | You can explain *why* it works from memory | You can define it but not explain it |
| **Discrimination** | You can separate it from a close non-example | You conflate it with a neighboring concept |
| **Transfer** | You can apply it to an unfamiliar problem | You only recognize textbook examples |
| **Boundaries** | You can state when it fails or doesn't apply | You overgeneralize |
| **Error diagnosis** | You can name the *type* of your mistake | Errors recur unexplained |

If a criterion fails, return to the phase that targets it—not to the beginning.

--
**Sticky phrase:** *"SPRINT the skeleton; DEEP the understanding."*

Use SPRINT when you need speed and recall. Use DEEP when the topic is conceptual or procedural and the goal is to explain, apply, and transfer—not just remember.
